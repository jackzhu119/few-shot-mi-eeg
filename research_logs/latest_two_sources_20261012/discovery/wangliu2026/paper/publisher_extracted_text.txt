Abstract
Motor imagery-based brain-computer interface (MI-BCI) applications in stroke rehabilitation aim to match brain activity with real-time feedback, thereby establishing closed-loop neural pathways and providing a basis for evaluating patients’ neuroplasticity changes. Thus, constructing EEG datasets under MI paradigms is crucial for optimizing MI-BCI systems and understanding the neural rehabilitation process. However, the current limitations of single MI paradigms and the lack of relevant EEG datasets may restrict the accurate interpretation and effective application in stroke rehabilitation. This study collected EEG data from 24 stroke patients during MI tasks, including a novel “sixth finger” MI and an affected-hand MI paradigm. The dataset comprehensively covers the complete longitudinal stages of stroke rehabilitation: pre-training, post-training, and follow-up periods. The data materials include: (1) raw EEG data, (2) preprocessed data, and (3) patient clinical information. Preliminary analysis using classical machine learning algorithms (CSP + SVM and CSP + LDA) demonstrated an average classification accuracy between the two MI paradigms maintained at approximately 85%~86%. We anticipate that this dataset will facilitate research on MI-BCI paradigms and neuroplasticity for stroke, and contribute to the development of high-efficiency MI-BCI systems in the field of stroke rehabilitation.

Background & Summary
Stroke results from a severe disruption of blood supply to the brain
1
, which can damage motor and sensory neural pathways
2
. The foundation of motor rehabilitation for stroke patients is neuroplasticity, a process through which undamaged neurons and cortical areas can compensate for or reorganize the functions previously managed by the injured brain regions
3
. Motor imagery-based brain–computer interface (MI-BCI) technology opens the door for establishing the closed-loop and enhancing motor relearning process. One of the main factors is that, a closed-loop MI-BCI system can estimate the motor intention of patients from the electroencephalography (EEG) generated by brain activity and output matching sensorimotor feedback
4
,
5
, which has gained consensus on the feasibility for stroke rehabilitation application. Another key factor in the utilization of MI for stroke rehabilitation is that MI has “movement property”
6
. MI can be approximated as a mental rehearsal of actual movement execution without actual motor outputs, with a high degree of similarity in neural activation patterns to motor execution (ME)
7
. The neural activation patterns during MI are often used to evaluate the effectiveness of neurological functional rehabilitation in patients.
Despite previous investigations into the theoretical feasibility of applying MI-BCI systems to stroke rehabilitation
8
,
9
, further research on MI paradigms is needed to improve rehabilitation outcomes, particularly through the establishment of MI databases specifically designed for stroke patients. However, currently available upper-limb motor imagery database studies for stroke patients remain relatively limited. Liu
et al
.
10
established the first MI dataset for acute stroke patients, which incorporated conventional upper-limb MI task paradigms. Previous studies have shown that, compared with healthy individuals, patients with stroke exhibit weaker and abnormal event-related desynchronization (ERD) phenomenon in the motor cortex when attempting to move their affected upper limb
11
. This indicates that the blockage of the patient’s inherent control pathway over the affected limb will inevitably impair the ERD phenomenon in the motor areas induced by traditional MI paradigm (patient imagines the movement of affected limb), which may limit rehabilitation efficacy. Therefore, developing innovative MI paradigms to enhance neural modulation patterns is crucial for achieving more effective rehabilitation in stroke patients.
Supernumerary robotic limbs (SRL) are emerging wearable robotic devices and exist in a novel shape independent of the human native limbs
12
,
13
, and have shown potential for promoting neural functional remodeling by innovating the morphological configuration of native human body
14
,
15
. Mehring
16
analyzed the neuromechanics of two participants with polydactyly who each had six fingers. The fMRI results demonstrated that the representation of the sixth finger in the primary sensorimotor cortices was distinct from the representations of all other fingers, a new and independent functional mapping region was developed to correspond to the sixth finger. And our previous work has proposed brain-controlled “sixth finger” robot, which combines SRL and BCI system
17
,
18
. The fMRI results demonstrated that after 4-week BCI-SRL training, significant functional connectivity increases in the sensorimotor region and prefrontal cortex, accompanied by functional reorganization of the sensorimotor network
19
. These results demonstrated the potential of SRL for neural remodeling. In addition, we have proposed a novel MI paradigm based on the “sixth-finger” and validated it in healthy subjects
20
, demonstrating broader and stronger brain region activation. In this dataset, we have further made publicly available the EEG data based on the “sixth-finger” MI (SF-MI) and affected-hand MI (AH-MI) paradigm collected from stroke patients. Furthermore, there is a notable lack of publicly available longitudinal MI datasets under the stroke therapy, which are essential for elucidating neural plasticity mechanisms in rehabilitation. Thus, innovating MI paradigms and establishing a multi-paradigm and longitudinal-training EEG dataset for stroke patients are critical steps toward bridging this gap and facilitating the development of high-efficiency MI-BCI systems for stroke recovery.
This study collected neurorehabilitation data from 24 stroke patients, including EEG recordings under three assessment time points (pre-training, post-training, and follow-up period), two motor imagery paradigms (SF-MI and AH-MI), and resting state. A total of 5,360 MI trials and 5,360 resting-state trials were acquired. The public dataset comprises three elements: raw EEG data, preprocessed data, and patient information. The preprocessed data can be used directly with the provided code to visualize results and conduct classification tasks. Researchers are also encouraged to repurpose the dataset for other investigations. To our knowledge, this constitutes the first large-scale MI dataset for stroke patients that combines multi-paradigm, including the “sixth-finger” and “affected-hand” MI, with longitudinal-training.
Therefore, the main contributions of this study are as follows: 1) a novel MI dataset for stroke patients is provided, comprising SF-MI and AH-MI, with a comparative analysis of EEG characteristics; 2) and longitudinal EEG data collected during stroke rehabilitation are provided, offering data support for understanding the neural plasticity mechanisms associated with MI-BCI interventions in stroke patients. Using this dataset, we conducted comparative analyses of EEG characteristics between SF-MI and AH-MI tasks in stroke patients, as well as pre- versus post-training assessments. Results show that the ERD activation during the novel SF-MI paradigm was stronger than traditional AH-MI paradigm. Based on classical machine learning algorithms (CSP + SVM and CSP + LDA), an average classification accuracy of 85%~86% was achieved, demonstrating comparable or even superior performance relative to existing public datasets, thus validating the reliability of the present dataset.
Method
Patients
This study recruited a total of 24 stroke patients from Tianjin Huanhu Hospital, including 18 males and 6 females. The patients ranged in age from 34 to 77 years, with a mean age of 55.75 years. Among them, 15 had right hemiplegia and 9 had left hemiplegia. And fourteen patients received brain-computer interface (BCI) training (BCI group), while 10 patients received conventional physical rehabilitation therapy (Traditional group). The BCI training involved a 2-week rehabilitation program using the brain-controlled “sixth-finger”
15
,
17
system independently developed by the research team from Tianjin University. The conventional physical training consisted of a 2-week rehabilitation program and administered by rehabilitation physicians from Tianjin Huanhu Hospital. The BCI group and traditional group received equivalent training dosages. Each intervention consisted of 8 training sessions over 2 weeks, with 4 sessions per week, and each session lasted approximately 45 min. In addition, all patients underwent three functional assessments: immediately before the intervention (pre-training), immediately after the intervention (post-training), and 1 month after the intervention (follow-up).
All participants provided written informed consent, confirming their agreement to participate in the study intervention and assessment procedures, the collection of relevant research data, and the public sharing of de-identified research data for scientific reuse. The study protocol adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Tianjin Huanhu Hospital (IRB committee number: IEC-B-003-V3.0). To protect participant information, sequential study codes were used in this work instead of participants’ names. Other information unrelated to the research but potentially capable of identifying individual participants was removed, ensuring that the publicly released information cannot be linked to any specific participant.
Patient eligibility was assessed by medical professionals, and the inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) aged 30–80 years; (2) first-ever stroke due to cerebral hemorrhage or infarction; (3) disease duration of 3–14 months; and (4) normal cognitive function, and ability to comprehend and perform experimental tasks. Exclusion criteria included: (1) severe psychological disorders or epilepsy that could interfere with task completion, and (2) scalp conditions preventing valid EEG acquisition. Experimental paradigms
The dataset includes three collection phases: the pre-training phase, collected before patients undergo a two-week training period; the post-training phase, collected immediately after patients complete the two-week training; and the follow-up phase, collected after a one-month washout period following the completion of the training. 19/24 patients participated in all EEG data collection phases, while 5/24 patients were absent from the follow-up data collection due to personal reasons. The data collection paradigm was consistent across all collection phases, as shown in Fig.
1(A)
. Each trial lasted 14 s and was initiated by pressing the space bar. A green fixation cross was first displayed at the center of the screen for 7 s, during which the patients remained in a resting state. This was followed by a gray circle presented for 1 second to prompt the patients to prepare for the MI task. Subsequently, MI period commenced for 6 s, during which a picture cue randomly appeared on the screen—either showing a clenched fist of the affected hand or a flexed “sixth-finger”—to indicate the type of AH-MI or SF-MI task in this trial. Therefore, this dataset comprises EEG signals from three paradigms: the rest task (events as Label 1), AH-MI task (events as Label 2), and SF-MI task (events as Label 3):

The subjects were asked to focus their minds on imagining the internal bodily sensations experienced when performing an action (kinesthetic imagery), rather than visualizing themselves performing the action from a third-person perspective as spectators of their own movements (visual imagery), while refraining from any physical movement during the MI tasks.
Data collection & preprocessing
EEG signals were recorded using a 64-channel NeuSen W system (Neuracle) with Ag/AgCl electrodes arranged according to the international 10–20 system. The reference and ground electrodes were placed at CPz and the forehead, respectively. Signals were sampled at 250 Hz and a 50 Hz notch filter was applied to suppress line noise.
Preprocessing was performed in EEGLAB. The steps included: (1) band-pass filtering between 5~35 Hz and application of a 50 Hz notch filter; (2) re-referencing to the common average reference (CAR); (3) removal of peripheral channels prone to motion artifacts, resulting in 40 channels for analysis shown in Fig.
1(C)
; (4) manual artifact rejection to identify and remove transient artifacts characterized by abnormal amplitude fluctuations at specific time points, with channels exhibiting persistent abnormal amplitude patterns interpolated; and (5) independent component analysis (ICA) to identify and remove components associated with eye-movement artifacts, based on scalp topographies, temporal waveforms.

Technical Validation
ERD/ERS analysis
The ERD
23
phenomenon is the main brain activation index for MI-BCI. event-related spectral perturbation (ERSP) was used to reflect the event-related power variations of the induced EEG signals in time and frequency domain
24
, which defined as follows:
$${ERSP}\left. (f,t\right)=\frac{1}{n}\mathop{\sum }\limits_{k=1}^{n}({F}_{k}{(f,t)}^{2})$$
where
n
is the number of trials, and
\({F}_{k}{(f,t)}^{2}\)
is the spectral estimation of
k
th trial at frequency
f
and time
t
. And in order to quantify the ERD phenomenon, the baseline correction was conducted by subtracting the average ERSP value within the baseline period (1 s before the start of the MI task), which defined as follows:
$${ERD}\left. (f,t\right)=\frac{1}{n}\mathop{\sum }\limits_{k=1}^{n}({{ERSP}}_{k}\left. (f,t\right)-{{Baseline}}_{k}(f))$$
$${{Baseline}}_{k}(f)=\frac{1}{m}\mathop{\sum }\limits_{t=1}^{m}({{ERSP}}_{k}\left. (f,t\right))$$
where
n
is the number of trials, and
\({{ERSP}}_{k}\left. (f,t\right)\)
is the ERSP value of
k
th trial at frequency
f
and time
t
, and
\({{Baseline}}_{k}\left(f\right)\)
is the average ERSP value within the baseline period,
m
is the number of time points within the baseline period.
The average ERD topographical maps in mu rhythm for different paradigm, group and collection phases were shown in Fig.
3(A)
. To account for variations in the hemiplegic side among patients (nine with left hemiplegia, the rest with right hemiplegia), we performed mirroring of the electrode, aligning the left side of the topographical map to represent the hemisphere contralateral to hemiplegia and the right side to represent the ipsilateral hemisphere. The results show that bilateral ERD activation in sensorimotor cortex was induced when patients performed MI tasks; and the ERD activation during the SF-MI paradigm was stronger than AH-MI paradigm. Additionally, patients in the BCI group exhibited a gradual weakening of ERD activation patterns after BCI rehabilitation training, with activation primarily concentrated near the sensorimotor cortex, reflecting enhanced neural efficiency. In contrast, patients in the Traditional group showed a gradual shift of ERD activation from the sensorimotor cortex toward the occipital lobe after conventional physical therapy.

Classification accuracy
In this study, each trial of the SF-MI and AH-MI tasks lasted 6 s. Based on the earlier finding that the ERD phenomenon tends to stabilize after 0.5~1 s, the EEG data segment from 1 s~5 s after the onset of the MI task was selected for classification. The extracted 4 s data segment from each trial was treated as an independent sample, thereby ensuring trial-level independence and preventing overly optimistic classification performance caused by temporal adjacency between samples. And other researchers may adopt customized sample-segmentation strategies according to their specific research objectives and analytical requirements. To demonstrate the inherent classifiability induced by the MI paradigm itself, this study employed classical machine learning algorithms for the binary classification task between SF-MI and AH-MI. EEG features were extracted using the Common Spatial Pattern (CSP) method, and the resulting feature vectors were subsequently classified using both support vector machine (SVM) and linear discriminant analysis (LDA) classifiers. A 10-fold cross-validation procedure was applied, in which the EEG data of each subject was randomly partitioned into 10 groups. For each fold, one group was used as the test set and the remaining nine groups as the training set. This process was repeated 10 times, and the average of the results was taken as the classification accuracy for each subject. The final classification accuracy was then computed as the mean across all subjects.
Figure
5
displays the binary classification accuracy results of the SF-MI and AH-MI paradigms for participants in the BCI group and the Traditional group during the pre-training, post-training, and follow-up phases. The accuracy rates for BCI group patients across these three phases were 85.09%, 85.59%, 85.78% under CSP + LDA and 85.63%, 85.57%, 86.42% under CSP + SVM, respectively, while the Traditional group patients achieved accuracy rates of 86.02%, 85.95%, 83.60% under CSP + LDA and 85.26%, 85.59%, 83.42% under CSP + SVM respectively. Compared to other unilateral limb multi-paradigm classification results typically ranging between 60%~70%
25
,
26
,
27
, these outcomes not only demonstrate superior performance but also show that all participants maintained relatively stable classification performance throughout the three phases, confirming the reliability of the present dataset.

Usage Notes
When using this dataset, it is important to consider the following: Due to individual patient circumstances, not all participants completed every experimental task. Specifically, subjects sub-05, sub-17, sub-18, sub-20 and sub-21 lack data from the follow-up sessions. Additionally, as this dataset includes patient data from multiple groups, appropriate adjustments to the code and data folder organization should be made when analyzing EEG data across different groups. To ensure de-identification of the raw data, all participant identifiers were replaced with sequential ID codes. Furthermore, any fields in the data files that could potentially contain identifiable information, including acquisition-site information, recording dates and names, were removed.
Code availability
All MATLAB scripts used in this study for preprocessing, feature extraction, plotting, and classification are available in the ‘code’ folder
21
. These scripts require users to install the EEGLAB toolbox in advance. The subject details, electrodes information, and event markers along with raw datasets and preprocessed datasets, have been made publicly available
