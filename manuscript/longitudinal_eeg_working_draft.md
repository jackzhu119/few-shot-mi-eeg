## Abstract

Brain–computer interface (BCI) performance depends on both a user's signals and the decoder mapping. We developed an auditable longitudinal EEG workflow using one NETBCI participant, with a separate summary of the published cohort's behavior. The verified NEMAR v1.0.0 derivative contained 717 right-hand motor-imagery or rest events across four sessions and 24 runs, with 74 EEG channels at 250 Hz. Original headers and markers corroborated identity while revealing label, sampling and event-duration differences. The original v2.2 behavior table provided 456 run percentages for 19 participants; mean session summaries increased from 54.11% to 68.68%, with a last-minus-first difference of +14.57 percentage points (paired participant-bootstrap 95% interval 10.71–18.27). Four participant/session EEG–behavior joins were established, but score–run order, scoring denominators and trial outcomes remained unresolved. The EEG participant ranked first in this behavioral cohort at all sessions. Exploratory covariance and spectral-subspace differences depended on quality-screening choices. Mu motor-imagery-minus-rest log-power contrasts became more negative across sessions, retaining the first-to-last direction under quality and balanced-trial sensitivities; these were task-window contrasts without a prestimulus baseline. A frozen CSP–LDA decoder achieved balanced accuracy 0.600 on the held-out reference-session set and 0.500 on all later-session sets, predicting rest for every later-session query. A three-epoch CPU EEGNet check yielded later-session values 0.557, 0.540 and 0.450, validating execution rather than definitive model performance. Independent computational replays reproduced saved outputs. Online hit rates, task-related signals and fixed-decoder transportability are distinct endpoints, based on different sample sets and mappings. These findings establish a traceable feasibility analysis and hypotheses for multi-participant follow-up; they do not identify neural learning, lost skill or learning-preserving adaptation. Such claims require reliable mapping/behavioral records and controlled online probes with retention measurements.

## Introduction

BCI control is often assessed through decoding accuracy or assisted task success. Both depend on the user–decoder system. A person may acquire a useful neural strategy while the decoder changes, or maintain the same strategy while signal acquisition changes. Conversely, a decoder may improve immediate task performance without establishing a durable ability to generate task-relevant neural activity under an independent control mapping. This distinction motivates the long-term question guiding our research programme: **How should brain–computer interfaces adapt without compromising human neural skill acquisition?** The present study addresses the measurement foundation needed to investigate that question.

Three recent studies provide complementary experimental ideas. Wang and colleagues combined tactile guidance, structured trial instructions, and EEGNet updates in a human motor-imagery BCI [1]. Their design included online tests without tactile stimulation and follow-up tests after more than two months. The study illustrates why training performance should be supplemented by tests that change the availability of guidance. However, removing tactile stimulation did not hold every decoder-related factor constant. In particular, weighted and conventional EEGNet models were reinitialized for each long-term-test session. These observations motivate independent retention probes; they do not justify equating their follow-up endpoint with performance under an unchanged initial decoder. The main Methods report random assignment, but the public reporting summary contains an inconsistent allocation statement; the execution of randomization remains unresolved in our literature audit.

Busch and colleagues manipulated the mapping between neural activity and avatar control in a real-time fMRI BCI [2]. Their within- and outside-manifold perturbations offered an intervention on the relationship between an existing neural activity space and a control mapping. Such experiments provide stronger evidence about learning constraints than a correlation between natural signal drift and classification accuracy. The modality and task also matter: fMRI navigation networks, haemodynamic timing, and voxel-derived embeddings do not establish the biological meaning of scalp EEG covariance subspaces. A further implication is that productive learning can alter performance under an old readout. Thus, a frozen decoder is an operational reference for transportability, not a complete definition of retained human skill.

Rajeswaran and colleagues examined neural representations under assistive decoder algorithms in invasive macaque BCIs [3]. Their analyses separated task-information compactness from overall variance and dimensionality, including task-relevant information in low-variance modes. This distinction cautions against treating stable leading principal components as sufficient evidence of preserved task representation. The animal adaptive-decoder data and historical fixed-decoder comparison involved different animals and decoder architectures; controlled network simulations supported a possible mechanism but did not establish causal effects on human EEG learning. EEG electrodes also record mixtures of neural and non-neural sources, so sensor-feature compactness cannot be interpreted as concentration of information in a known number of neurons.

Together, these studies suggest a measurement strategy that retains three distinct quantities: task performance, the structure and task information of neural features, and the decoder's changing or fixed mapping. The contribution sought here is an auditable implementation and a bounded feasibility assessment of that strategy. We first verify the source-to-event chain, then determine the behavioural grain actually supported by released records, and finally evaluate exploratory representations and source-frozen decoders. We do not reproduce the three interventions or propose that borrowing a geometry metric constitutes algorithmic novelty.

NETBCI is relevant because its source release describes 19 participants studied on four different days, performing right-hand motor imagery or rest during cursor-feedback training [4–6]. Our downloaded EEG subset contains only participant `sub-1`, corresponding to original `sub-01`. We ask whether this subset supports a reliable longitudinal analysis pipeline, whether released scores can be joined to EEG at an appropriate grain, and what failures appear when a source-trained decoder is transported to later sessions. Hypotheses about representation–performance dissociation, drift–transportability associations, and the causal effects of adaptation remain future tests requiring more evidence.

## Methods

### Study scope and evidence provenance

This was an exploratory secondary analysis of one participant. No new participants were recruited, no experimental mapping was manipulated, and no whole-cohort download was undertaken. The analyzed signal source was the NEMAR `nm000305` EDF/BIDS derivative, pinned to v1.0.0 and its version DOI [5]. Its metadata declare MOABB 1.8.0dev0 as the generator; an upstream retrieval record names a separate MOABB development revision. Neither was conflated with the locally installed MOABB 1.4.3, and the complete converter revision was not independently reconstructed. The original Dataverse release was audited separately at version 2.2 [6]. Original BrainVision signals, MEG signals, and MRI were not combined with the derivative EEG.

The local signal audit checked 169 downloaded files comprising 24 EDF recordings and associated metadata, totalling 193,462,298 bytes. Official manifest checksums were interpreted according to their declared schemes: signal-file SHA-256 and small-file Git-blob SHA-1 were not conflated. Header, marker, event, channel, and session metadata from the original archive were obtained through bounded HTTP range reads. Selected ZIP members passed CRC checks and were given SHA-256 receipts. Because the entire archive was not downloaded, its whole-file checksum was not independently verified. Original-archive indexes were inspected for released behavioural and session-record candidates; absence from inspected material was not treated as proof that no additional author-held records exist.

Each stored epoch retained participant, session, run, semantic event label, event-table row, source-file path, onset, stored duration, and sample boundaries. A stable row identifier such as `sub-1/ses-01/run-01/tsv-row-1` identifies a row in the released event table. It does not invent a missing original experimental trial identifier. The complete event inventory provides corresponding original-file paths and hashes, original timestamps and samples, and derivative event values. The adapter did not interpolate missing trials or attach behavioural outcomes.

The stage-1 receipt records the data-bundle SHA-256, metadata SHA-256, configuration and script SHA-256, runtime versions, random seed, and source-tree hashes. The recorded base Git commit was `a15c3622883e9939d5b57e7bd888d2fe0c7d64a4`; the tree was dirty at execution, so that commit alone is insufficient to identify the analysis code. The accompanying source hashes identify the executed files. Primary results are in `research_logs/netbci_stage1_20261010/run01/`; `run02_replay/` preserves a separate computational replay. Post-observation sensitivity results are identified as such rather than described as preregistered analyses.

### Event semantics, chronology, and epoch construction

The task was **right-hand motor imagery versus rest**, not left- versus right-hand imagery. In original-release metadata, task codes were right-hand imagery 1 and rest 2; the derivative stored right-hand imagery 2 and rest 1. Labels were resolved from the actual task fields, source coding evidence, and EDF annotations rather than a hard-coded numeric assumption. TSV onset and duration fields used seconds. Original BrainVision marker positions were one-based, while event-table samples were zero-based. All matched marker positions were checked using that documented difference.

For 23 original recordings, the header rate was 250 Hz. Original session 01/run 04 reported 249.89999389648438 Hz, independently consistent between the header sampling interval and EEG sidecar. Its derivative was 250 Hz. Rounded derivative event samples matched the original onset expressed at the derivative rate; the maximum onset difference was approximately 1.971 ms. This verifies timing consistency with the reported rate conversion, not equality of original and exported signal values or full reconstruction of the converter's resampling implementation.

Original event durations were zero-duration markers. The derivative provided five-second task windows. The inspected original [BIDS-conversion code at a pinned revision](https://github.com/mccorsi/NETBCI_data/blob/48fe2b437ac641d222ad5c214f26f3008204ebd0/scripts/bidsify/BIDSify.py) describes `[0, 5]` s as target plus feedback, with an inter-stimulus phase `[-1, 0]` s and result phase `[5, 7]` s. We retained the five seconds as an exported task-window convention, not a measured time-to-target, a verified pure-imagery interval, or a successful-control period. Separate feedback onsets within that interval were not established. Epochs were exact half-open slices `[start_sample, start_sample + 1250)` at 250 Hz, producing the array `717 × 74 × 1250` in volts. Event uniqueness, ordering, annotation agreement, finite samples, non-overlap, and recording bounds were checked. The same 717 task events were already present in the original released event tables and markers; their count was not reduced by the observed NEMAR conversion. Reasons for absent design trials or missing rest events remain unresolved.

Sessions were ordered 01–04 using the published acquisition protocol and session labels. Original scan tables contain anonymized dates in 1916. We preserve these source fields but do not claim verified real calendar dates or actual inter-session intervals. Results are therefore reported by ordered session rather than by a reconstructed elapsed-day variable.

### Behavioural records and linkage criteria

Original `participants.tsv` and its dictionary were inspected alongside EEG metadata and archive inventories. The source participant row contained columns `BCI-Performance-session1` through `BCI-Performance-session4`, each with six reported percentages. The dictionary described percentages of trials successfully reaching the target during each run. Participant identity was supported by original-header hashes and source provenance; session identity was explicit in the column names.

A run-level join required an explicit run identifier, a documented vector-to-run mapping, or independently matching timestamps. None was established. Consequently, the verified behaviour table retains `vector_position` while setting `eeg_run`, `eeg_trial`, and `denominator` to `unresolved`. Candidate position-to-run assignments were kept in a separate audit artifact and were not used as verified analysis joins. No percentage was converted into hit/miss outcomes, no outcome was inferred from decoder predictions, and no missing trial was synthesized.

As a diagnostic, reported percentage precision was compared with the numbers of available EEG events under hypothetical candidate run ordering. Arithmetic compatibility does not establish the denominator or ordering. The analysis also considered whether records contained feedback onset, cursor trajectories, rejection reasons, or trial outcomes. None was validated in the inspected release material. Session-level unweighted means of the six reported percentages can be described without knowing their order, but they are neither pooled success rates nor trial-weighted estimates with known binomial denominators.

A subsequent exploratory analysis actually calculated these session summaries for all 19 published participants and joined the four EEG sessions of the verified participant at participant/session grain. The original source and dictionary MD5 checksums, historical feature/event identities, and preserved stage-1 hashes were rechecked. Complete four-session participant trajectories, rather than runs or trials, were bootstrapped 10,000 times (seed 42) for the mean last-minus-first change. Only one participant had EEG in this analysis. Published-score comparisons used a 1e−10 percentage-point numerical tolerance, with a separate 0.01-point reporting-precision sensitivity. No run-order assumption, denominator inference, trial outcome reconstruction, or neural–behavioural correlation significance test was used.

The same extension compared task-window mu/beta power by taking log10 of each trial's C3/Cz/C4 ROI bandpower, averaging within each class/run, and then equally weighting runs. The MI-minus-rest difference was multiplied by ten for a dB contrast. Arithmetic-power ratios were saved separately. Common-run (03–06), unflagged and class/run-balanced 96-trial sensitivities were recorded. These are between-task contrasts without a prestimulus baseline, not ERD/ERS or pure-imagery causal effects. This extension followed observation of the pilot results and remains exploratory.

### Preprocessing and source-fitted quality screening

Channel names and order were held common across recordings. Spectral analysis used all-channel common-average reference (CAR) and the central four seconds of each stored window, `[0.5, 4.5)` s. Welch power spectral density (PSD) used a periodic Hann window, 250-sample segments, 125-sample overlap, constant detrending, and density scaling. PSD units were V²/Hz; bandpower was obtained by trapezoidal integration over 8–13 Hz (mu) and 13–30 Hz (beta). C3, Cz, and C4 formed the specified descriptive region of interest. The shared 13-Hz boundary follows the saved configuration. A source-reference comparison was saved because rereferencing can materially change power estimates.

Decoder and covariance inputs were CAR signals filtered independently within each five-second epoch using a fourth-order Butterworth 8–30-Hz bandpass and zero-phase filtering, then cropped by 0.5 s at each edge. These offline transforms were not presented as a realizable online latency pipeline. A fixed 73-dimensional Helmert basis represented the CAR subspace, avoiding a data-estimated rank-reduction mapping. No spatial alignment or normalization was fitted to target-session test data for decoding.

Quality screening used the maximum channel peak-to-peak range in microvolts for each original epoch. A log-range threshold was fitted to the 90-trial source training partition as the source median plus six scaled median absolute deviations. An epoch was also flagged if any channel's within-epoch standard deviation was below 0.1 µV. Flags were retained with event identity; the primary analyses did not silently discard them. This is a coarse source-fitted screen, not expert artifact rejection or certification that retained signals are artifact-free. No ICA-based ocular/muscle removal or expert clinical review was performed.

Time-resolved event-relative PSD was computed separately with one-second windows and half-second steps. Without a validated prestimulus baseline or independently verified feedback onset, it was not reported as baseline-normalized ERD/ERS or as a feedback-locked physiological response.

### Exploratory longitudinal geometry

Spatial covariance matrices were estimated from filtered Helmert-coordinate epochs, demeaned in time, divided by their trace, and regularized using a fixed 0.05 shrinkage toward the 73-dimensional isotropic matrix. This produced positive-definite matrices suitable for affine-invariant Riemannian distance (AIRM). Session comparisons used arithmetic means of trial covariance matrices; these were not described as Riemannian barycentres. The distance was the Euclidean norm of the logarithms of generalized covariance eigenvalues. Trace normalization separated this analysis from absolute amplitude changes measured by bandpower.

To reduce differences in class and run sample counts, 12 epochs per class per run were sampled without replacement for each of the six runs, yielding 144 epochs per session. Twenty specified seeds (42–61) quantified sensitivity to this matching procedure. AIRM compared each session's mean covariance with the matched session-01 mean. Mean pairwise run covariance distances characterized within-session variation. These distances were descriptive; no null distribution or participant-level significance test was constructed.

Log mu/beta powers from all 74 channels formed a 148-feature vector. Standardization and the predictive reference PCA were fitted to the final 120-trial source fitting set only; the component count was fixed at 10. For a separate descriptive subspace comparison, 10-component PCAs were fitted within each matched session in the common source-standardized feature coordinates. Their normalized projection distance was based on principal angles, ranging from zero for identical subspaces to one for orthogonal subspaces. Target-session PCA fitting was restricted to this descriptive comparison and did not enter decoder fitting or model selection. These spectral-feature subspaces were not equated with neuronal or fMRI intrinsic manifolds.

Post-observation quality sensitivity used only unflagged epochs and the same eligible run IDs in every session. Runs 03–06 supplied at least 12 epochs per class/run across sessions, giving 96 matched epochs per session. Matching and covariance distances were repeated across the same 20 seeds. This analysis checks one screening/sample-control sensitivity; it is not a second biological replication or confirmation selected independently of the initial results.

### Time-ordered fixed-decoder evaluation

The run partition was fixed before the reported stage-1 evaluation. In session 01, runs 01–03 provided 90 development-training trials, run 04 provided 30 validation trials, and runs 05–06 provided 60 held-out reference-query trials. After source-only development, final decoders were newly fitted to the 120 training-plus-validation trials. Later-session run 01 was reserved as a potential calibration pool and was unused in frozen-decoder fitting; later-session runs 02–06 supplied 149, 150, and 148 query trials for sessions 02, 03, and 04. No target data or labels were used to select decoder parameters. Reference validation scores are reported as workflow checks, not as a search over the final test results.

CSP–LDA used four MNE CSP components with Ledoit–Wolf regularization and log average-power features, followed by shrinkage LDA with the `lsqr` solver. EEGNet used the installed Braindecode implementation with training-only channel centering/scaling, Adam at learning rate 0.001, batch size 16, seed 42, and three CPU epochs. EEGNet's three-epoch budget was deliberately a minimum real-data workflow check. There was no convergence assessment, seed ensemble, or validated hyperparameter comparison with CSP–LDA. Both fitted models were frozen for all query sessions, including normalization and evaluation-mode network state. Parameter hashes were checked before and after query inference, and predictions were replayed.

Balanced accuracy (BA), the mean of rest and right-hand recall, was reported with class-specific confusion matrices. Conditional percentile intervals resampled complete query runs with replacement 2,000 times. These intervals condition on one participant, the fitted model, the available sessions, and the sampled runs. They are not participant-population confidence intervals and do not include model-training uncertainty. Only two reference-query runs and five later-session runs were available per interval, limiting its precision and interpretability. A constant predictor can produce a degenerate bootstrap interval; this was reported as a failure case rather than certainty about a population score.

### Computational environment and reproducibility

The saved analysis used Python 3.11.16, NumPy 2.2.6, SciPy 1.15.3, scikit-learn 1.7.2, MNE 1.10.2, PyTorch 2.8.0+cpu, and Braindecode 1.2.0, with two computational threads. Independent analysis invocation reproduced the event inventory, trial features, run features, matched geometry, predictions, and decoder results exactly at the saved-output hash level. This establishes computational reproducibility for these outputs in this environment. It does not establish replication across people, devices, or independently acquired datasets.

## Results

### Verified data comprise one participant's four-session task recordings

The verified subset contained 717 released task events, consisting of 360 right-hand imagery and 357 rest events. The 24 continuous EDF recordings totalled 5,203 s (86.72 min); non-overlapping stored task windows totalled 3,585 s (59.75 min). All derivative recordings had a stable 74-channel order and 250-Hz sampling. Source and derivative event sequences agreed after their numeric-code and sample-origin differences were resolved. No duplicate task event or out-of-bounds stored window was detected by the specified checks.

File integrity and event alignment establish usable recorded data; they do not establish that all epochs are free from artifacts or that all originally performed trials were released.

### Behavioural linkage stops at participant/session identity

Twenty-four percentages were retained from the four explicitly named session-performance fields. Participant identity and source-session fields were traceable. Their association with particular EEG run numbers could not be verified. Trial hits/misses, target-hit timestamps, cursor trajectories, and actual scoring denominators were not established. Under the unverified candidate vector-to-run assignment, 23 of 24 percentages were incompatible with the corresponding 29- or 30-event EEG count at the stated printing precision. Several common hypothetical denominators were numerically compatible; none was selected as true.

The unweighted means of the six reported percentages were 73.8%, 90.48%, 85.08%, and 89.28% for sessions 01–04. These preserve the source's reported-score grain and do not require assuming a vector order. They should not be interpreted as reconstructed trial success rates, independent-control outcomes, or evidence of retention. No trial-level behaviour–EEG correlation and no verified run-level score–geometry association were computed. Four session aggregates from one participant do not provide a reliable statistical test of neural learning or a stable score–drift relationship.

### Published behavior can be linked at session grain despite unresolved trial outcomes

The published behavior table contained 19 unique participants, 76 complete participant/session records and 456 finite percentages. Cohort means of the six-run session summaries were 54.11%, 56.74%, 62.15% and 68.68%. The last-minus-first mean change was +14.57 percentage points (paired participant-bootstrap 95% interval 10.71–18.27). Eighteen participants increased at the last session and one decreased. Only four trajectories increased strictly at every transition; fourteen contained a decrease in published means, including one decrease below 0.01 point. This is an observational change under the historical training and decoder protocol, not an identified learning or adaptation effect.

The EEG participant's session scores were 73.80%, 90.48%, 85.08% and 89.28%, ranking first in this behavior cohort at every session. This unusually high-scoring single EEG pilot is not representative evidence for the other participants. Four participant/session joins were established; source positions remained unassigned to EEG runs or trials. Online hit scores summarize six reported run percentages, whereas frozen-decoder queries cover two source-session runs and five later-session runs. These different endpoints and sample sets cannot be subtracted as learning gain.

Equal-run mu MI-minus-rest log-power contrasts were −0.792, −1.058, −1.857 and −2.197 dB; common-run unflagged values were −0.776, −1.135, −1.886 and −2.102 dB. Under 20 class/run-balanced unflagged 96-trial subsamples per session, corresponding means were −0.848, −1.083, −1.904 and −2.084 dB. These subsamples retained the first-to-last direction but did not supply population confidence intervals or biological replication. Importantly, absolute geometric-mean mu power increased in both tasks from first to last session (rest 3.103 to 5.925 µV²; imagery 2.585 to 3.572 µV²); a more negative relative contrast did not establish an absolute imagery-power decrease. Beta contrasts were not similarly monotonic. Task cues, feedback, recording quality and session decoder changes remain possible contributors. Numerical results and all source-grain tables are in the [session behavior analysis](../docs/netbci_behavior_session_analysis.md).

### Frozen CSP–LDA failed to transport discrimination to later sessions

CSP–LDA's source validation BA was 0.633, and the final source-fit decoder reached BA 0.600 on the 60-event held-out reference-session set. Its BA was 0.500 in all later-session query sets. Confusion matrices showed that it predicted rest for all 447 later-session queries, including all 225 imagery events. This was a constant-class transport failure, not evidence that the participant's neural skill disappeared. Removing source-screened query epochs did not change that constant-class result.

EEGNet's development source-validation BA was 0.733, but its final source-fit reference-query BA was 0.500. Later-session values were 0.557, 0.540, and 0.450. The short training budget, single seed, small source fitting set, and broad conditional intervals preclude a superiority or convergence claim. Model-state hashes and repeated predictions were unchanged during all query evaluation. The completed workflow reported no execution exceptions, although scientifically material predictive failures remained. CSP–LDA and EEGNet inference and feature outputs were reproduced in a second analysis invocation; the result is computational repeatability, not evidence that an EEG change or learning effect will replicate in another participant.

## Discussion

This study establishes that a public NETBCI single-participant subset can support traceable session/run/trial identity, cross-version event auditing, descriptive geometry, and source-frozen decoder evaluation. The substantive preliminary result is a combination of recorded-feature differences, limited behavioural resolution, and a conspicuous transport failure. Those observations define the next measurement tasks more clearly than they support a learning-preservation claim.

### Distinguishing recorded-feature change from neural learning

Representational distances were reproducible by computational replay and were not eliminated by one source-fitted quality screen. That is useful evidence that the outputs are not accidental file-order or nondeterministic-computation artifacts. It does not isolate the origin of session differences. The quality sensitivity also changed their relative ordering. Multiple acquisition and task factors can alter scalp EEG geometry, and the experiment's online recalibration may influence what strategies participants use. Missing behavioural timing and score–run correspondence prevent determining whether a geometric change followed improved control, changed feedback, or a recording difference.

The representation question should therefore be sharpened before expansion. A confirmatory follow-up should measure class-conditioned geometry, split-half or repeat-run reliability, and task-predictive information in both high- and lower-variance components. It should retain reference and channel conventions, quantify artifact sensitivities, and distinguish changes in amplitude from trace-normalized covariance structure. These extensions follow the distinction between manifold constraints [2] and task-information concentration [3]; neither paper establishes that minimum EEG drift is an optimal objective.

### A frozen readout is a reference, not the human skill itself

The CSP–LDA confusion matrices reveal why an aggregate score alone is inadequate. Later-session BA of 0.500 was produced by predicting one class everywhere. It cannot tell whether task-related neural information was absent, whether a learned discriminant moved relative to a fixed threshold, whether preprocessing was suboptimal, or whether a task-relevant source changed. Those mechanisms require additional source-only planned baselines, diagnostic output inspection, and independently evaluated recalibration experiments. They cannot be resolved by relabelling outcomes or tuning the frozen model on its test sessions.

A frozen decoder remains valuable because it measures performance under a constant analytical mapping. However, preserving an initial mapping can conflict with beneficial new neural strategies. The real-time fMRI perturbation study's additional fixed-readout analysis is particularly relevant to this distinction [2]: the Supplementary Fig. 15 comparison of the original location readout after within-manifold training was marginal at one-sided P ≈ 0.0501, with an interval spanning zero. This suggests a question worth testing rather than proving deterioration or a universal trade-off. A scientifically meaningful retention endpoint should combine independent task performance, neural strategy evidence, and carefully defined frozen probes rather than requiring every learner to remain within an original readout. Similarly, the no-tactile and long-term tests in [1] motivate controls for guidance and persistence but cannot be imported as equivalent endpoints into an offline right-hand/rest dataset.

### What would constitute an independent research contribution?

The present workflow's audit trail is a necessary foundation, not an established conceptual innovation. An eventual independent contribution could be a validated separation of immediate decoder benefits from later independent control, accompanied by reliable task-information and mapping-compatibility measurements in human EEG. It would need to distinguish a policy that preserves useful controllable directions from a blanket penalty on all representational change. It should also permit beneficial plasticity, rather than prespecifying that stable geometry or fewer informative modes are desirable.

Original methods and author-code versions must be reconciled before reuse. Our close reading found differences between [1]'s published weighting/training description and the inspected public update implementation, and an unresolved repeated-split count difference within [3]. The inspected illustrative unit-ranking code in [3] also ranks using test scores, raising a selection issue if copied directly into an independent predictive evaluation; the complete original pipeline was not available to settle its applicability. A future EEG information-adding analysis should rank features within inner training/validation partitions and evaluate them on an independent outer run or participant test set. These observations define safeguards for our experiment, rather than claims that the published studies have been refuted.

Candidate hypotheses are presently conditional. H1 predicts that changes in EEG representation need not parallel changes in decoding or reported online scores; the current patterns motivate this question but do not establish a cohort-level dissociation. H2 predicts an association between specified representation changes and frozen-reference transportability; four sessions in one participant, with a constant later-session shallow decoder, are insufficient for a reliable association test. H3 predicts that policies maximizing immediate decoding can differ in subsequent independent-probe outcomes. H4 predicts that appropriately constrained mappings can improve longer-term stability. The current observational offline analysis cannot test H3 or H4 because it contains neither assigned competing adaptation policies nor independent withdrawal and delayed-retention outcomes.

### Prospective design needed for the learning-preserving programme

A future human experiment should compare fixed, conventional adaptive, and candidate adaptation policies under the same decoder family, labelled-data budget, task, training duration, and feedback conditions. Random assignment or a justified counterbalanced design should address carry-over; neural-control learning may make a simple crossover unsuitable. Decoder versions, update times, sensory feedback, cursor states, targets, task events, trial validity, and scoring denominators should be logged synchronously. The candidate policy must be specified before definitive testing and supported by independent development data.

Training performance should be measured alongside scheduled independent probes, withdrawal of extra algorithmic assistance, delayed retention, and transfer where appropriate. Two mappings must be distinguished. Freezing the **currently learned mapping** at the end of training and reusing it without extra assistance or further updates at immediate and delayed tests measures retention of that acquired control. Reintroducing the **initial reference mapping** measures transfer or readability under an earlier readout, which can change even if a new control skill was retained. A probe's mapping and recalibration rules must be explicit so that intervention effects cannot be absorbed into the evaluation decoder. Human task outcomes should be primary for a claim about skill acquisition or retention; neural geometry and model accuracy can supply complementary mechanistic or operational endpoints. Participant-level sample size, clinically or scientifically meaningful margins, and analysis power must be planned from appropriate pilot variability, not calculated by treating hundreds of trials as hundreds of people. No participant recruitment, new intervention, sample-size claim, or ethics application is presented as completed here.

## Limitations

The EEG component is a one-participant feasibility study with one fitting seed and four ordered sessions. The separate 19-participant behavior-only summary does not enlarge the EEG sample or establish cohort neural–behavioural association. The EEG analysis cannot estimate population neural effects or subject-specific neural variability across people. Twenty resamplings and an exact computational replay do not increase the number of independent participants.

The derivative covers released imagery/rest runs, not the full multimodal dataset or all originally performed trials. Original continuous signals were not compared with exported signals. Conversion-generated time windows, imperfectly specified original reference/acquisition details, and anonymized calendar dates constrain physiology and elapsed-time interpretation. The inspected public metadata do not establish EEG-linked trial outcomes, run-score order, or original exclusion rules.

Quality control was coarse, source-fitted screening rather than expert review. Spectral and geometry results depend on channel/reference/filter/window choices. Session-specific descriptive PCA fits are distinguishable from training-only predictive fitting, but neither supplies a validated intrinsic neuronal manifold. No task-information compactness analysis, baseline-normalized ERD/ERS, split-half measurement-reliability model, or intervention-specific null test was completed.

The reference-query set had two runs, and each later query set had five. Run-bootstrap intervals are conditional and omit participant and training uncertainty. EEGNet was intentionally trained for only three epochs; the experiment cannot rank architectures or demonstrate that decoder transport fails generally. The shallow model's observed constant-class failure requires diagnosis rather than a claim that human control deteriorated.

The literature comparison draws on heterogeneous modalities, species, tasks, and controls. The accessible geometry-study evidence includes its publisher record and public supplementary/source materials; access limitations and exact reading scope are recorded separately. None of the three cited studies was reproduced here. No causal adaptation effect, human learning preservation, or superior candidate algorithm has been shown.
